The definition the Claude Code team actually uses
An agent doing rounds of work until a condition you set becomes true.

A prompt

Asks for the next thing. It does it, tells you, and stops. You judge it.

A goal

Names something that has to be true. It keeps going until it is — or until it runs out of turns or money.

The only real differencewho decides the work is finished
Say it, then pause. The words that matter are "a condition you set" — everything hard about this lives there. The line that lands: "When you prompt, you are the stop condition. A loop just means you had to write it down." That's usually the moment people realise this is harder than prompting, not easier.
The same shape whether it runs for one minute or all night

What's actually happening inside

It finishedyou get the answer, the cost, and a session you can pick up again
It ran out of turnsno answer at all — resume it or raise the cap
It ran out of moneyyour spend cap stopped it, helpers counted in
It broke, or it said noalways check why before you trust what came out
Three of those four are not successmost people design the happy path and let the rest surprise them at 3am
Trace the loop once with the dot: a prompt goes in, it decides what to do, it runs a tool, the result comes straight back to it, and round it goes. The only way out is a turn where it doesn't want a tool. That's the whole mechanism — everything else in this talk is about what you put around it. Then the real point: three of those four endings are not success. Most people design the happy path and let the other three surprise them at three in the morning. These are literally the four results the SDK hands back, and the answer text only exists on the first one. "If your failure path is a Slack message nobody reads, that's not a loop, that's a leak."

Four kinds of loop, and what you give up in each

You give up — checking it

Turn by turn

Every prompt already loops — you're just the one judging each round. Write your checks into a skill and it can judge itself.

Use it when you're still working out what you want
You give up — deciding when to stop

Goal  /goal

Say what done looks like. A faster model checks after every round and sends it back if you're not there yet.

Use it when done is something you can measure
You give up — starting it

On a timer  /loop /schedule

Same job, new inputs each time. Or you're watching something outside your control — a queue, a channel — and reacting when it changes.

Use it when the work turns up on a clock, not from you
You give up — the prompt itself

Left running

All three, together. It wakes up, finds the work, fixes it, and only brings you what it's unsure about.

What it actually takes to get from prompt to a shipped outcome

Needed — 1

Guardrails mined from your own work

Rules pulled from your own review history, not the model's generic defaults. An agent judged against your team's real standard earns trust faster than one judged against a style guide nobody reads.

Needed — 2

One path, wired end to end

CLI, API, MCP — connect decision to action all the way through. Every handoff you leave for a human by hand is a place where the autonomy quietly stops.

Needed — 3

Metrics on the outcome, not the activity

Lines of code and PR counts stop meaning anything once an agent writes most of both. Track what shipped and stuck — not what was typed.

None of this is optionalskip one pillar and the other two just produce more code, faster — not more trust
This is the checklist, not a story — three things that actually decide whether a loop reaches production or stays a demo. Needed 1 is the trust problem: an agent that only knows generic best practices will always feel unsafe to hand real work to. Needed 2 is the unglamorous truth — most of what makes a loop production-ready is plumbing, not model choice. Needed 3 is the trap almost everyone falls into first: measuring activity because it's easy to count, right up until an agent can generate infinite activity. Land the foot line slowly — these three don't trade off against each other, they compound.

If you remember two ideas from the whole talk, make it these

Never let it mark its own homework

  • The agent that wrote the code is a soft marker. It already believes its own reasoning.
  • A second agent starting fresh catches what the first one talked itself into.
  • Prefer checks a machine can settle — tests, types, a score. Cheaper, and harder to argue with.
  • Give it a hard problem and a visible test, and it will sometimes fix the test instead.

It forgets. Your repo doesn't.

  • Long runs get summarised as the window fills — including instructions you gave at the start.
  • So the rules live in files it re-reads every run, not in your opening message.
  • Keep what's done, what failed and what's next on disk. That's what lets tomorrow carry on from today.
  • Try it: kill the run halfway. If tomorrow can't tell where today stopped, you haven't got a loop.
Why the checker matters mosta loop runs while you're not watching, so the check is the only reason you can walk away
Analogy that always works for the left side: reviewing your own pull request at six on a Friday. Then sharpen it — the agent isn't lying, it's just working from its own assumptions, so a stern prompt won't fix it. A clean slate will. On the test-gaming point, don't say "the AI cheats" — say "whatever you measure is what it optimises, so measure the outcome and not the proxy." On the right side, lean into how unglamorous it is: "the most advanced part of your autonomous system is going to be a text file, and you'll feel silly about that for about a week."

What happens when people actually run these for a month

Four ways this goes wrong, and one fair argument against it

  • The bill. Helpers multiply the calls. Loops left running overnight with no cap have run up four-figure bills.
  • It wanders. Long runs drift off the goal and still report success. Plenty of people got better work by staying involved.
  • You stop knowing your own code. The faster it ships work you didn't write, the wider that gap gets.
  • Review jams up. An MIT study this year found far more code written and only a fraction more shipped. Writing stopped being the hard part.

The argument against

One engineer's take: the loop was a stopgap while the tools caught up. /goal is the proof — what you hand-built now ships as one command.

He's partly right. Most things people call loops are cron jobs and webhooks with a model in the middle.

What survives: something still has to define done, track progress, and decide when to stop. That didn't vanish into the command — it moved into your design.

Say this straight, with no defensiveness — the credibility you buy here is what makes the last section land. Have your own number ready for what a nightly loop costs you a week. If you haven't got one, say so and tell them to measure before they scale. On the sceptic: agree with him in public, then draw the line — the command got easier, the design didn't. Anyone who has written a stop condition that actually stops knows the difference.

What's coming in the next year

Checking gets formal

Eval engineering

Your checker becomes a versioned test set you score against, built partly from real failures. Expect this to become a job title within the year.

One loop becomes many

Graph engineering

A loop is one agent's behaviour; a graph is the org chart joining many. Right now, one strong planner directing cheaper workers wins on cost.

Running lots of them at once

Fleet engineering

Once you're running dozens, the questions get operational: who owns this, what it cost, who can turn it off. Registries, budgets, a kill switch.

The organisation catches up

Everything downstream

Review and release, rebuilt around agent output instead of typing speed. Logins and permissions for things that aren't people. Per-seat pricing gets odd once the seats aren't staff.

Notice the patternevery one of these is a way to control something we already made too fast
Roughly 50 seconds each. Anchor each one in something real rather than a prediction: eval tooling already has a vendor market, graph engineering got named in July, fleet tooling exists today, and the last one is what Anthropic describes doing internally. If someone asks which matters most for their team, say eval engineering without hesitating — it's the one that makes everything above it safe. Also worth saying plainly: don't chase the layer being talked about online. Fix the one you're standing on.

Two things to say plainly. First, it isn't one score — your docs pipeline can sit at step 3 while anything touching payments stays at step 1, and picking the right level per job is the maturity.
